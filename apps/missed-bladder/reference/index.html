The Name Explained

What Does MISSED Mean?

"Missed Bladder Disease" represents four categories of bladder conditions in women that are chronically underdiagnosed, undertreated, and overlooked in clinical practice — despite their profound impact on quality of life.

M

Missed

Bladder conditions that go undiagnosed because symptoms like burning, frequency, and pelvic pain are attributed to other causes such as infection, anxiety, or gynaecological disorders.

I

Ignored

Symptoms that are minimized by patients or clinicians as "normal for women" — including chronic urinary urgency, suprapubic pressure, and persistent dysuria.

S

Suppressed

Conditions managed symptomatically with repeated antibiotics or analgesia without addressing the underlying bladder pathology — the defective GAG layer.

T

Trivialised

Chronic bladder pain dismissed as psychosomatic or exaggerated, delaying diagnosis by years and allowing progression to severe, treatment-refractory disease.

Conditions Under the Missed Bladder Disease Umbrella

  • Interstitial Cystitis / Bladder Pain Syndrome (IC/BPS) — the primary chronic condition characterized by bladder pain, urgency, and frequency without identifiable cause.
  • Recurrent Urinary Tract Infections (rUTI) — persistent recurrence often masking underlying GAG layer deficiency or bladder wall vulnerability.
  • Hemorrhagic Cystitis — bladder bleeding arising from radiation, chemotherapy, or immune triggers, causing significant morbidity.
  • Radiation Cystitis — late-onset bladder damage following pelvic radiotherapy for cervical, endometrial, or rectal cancers.
  • Chemotherapy-Induced Cystitis — cyclophosphamide and BCG-induced urothelial damage requiring protective mucosal support.
  • Neurogenic Bladder — bladder dysfunction from neurological causes where altered innervation leads to storage and voiding dysfunction.
  • Bladder Sensitivity Syndrome — early-stage bladder hypersensitivity before formal IC/BPS diagnosis, representing the greatest prevention window.

Disease Mechanism

Pathophysiology of IC / Bladder Pain Syndrome

The underlying pathology of IC/BPS centers on disruption of the protective mucosal barrier — the glycosaminoglycan (GAG) layer — and the cascade of events that follow.

Step 1 — Initiation

GAG Layer Disruption

The glycosaminoglycan layer — a negatively charged mucus lining the urothelium — becomes defective. This may be triggered by recurrent UTIs, hormonal changes, trauma, or autoimmune processes. The protective barrier loses integrity, exposing underlying tissue.

Step 2 — Penetration

Urinary Irritant Infiltration

With the GAG layer compromised, urinary constituents — potassium, toxins, and bacteria — penetrate the urothelium into the suburothelial space. Potassium depolarizes nerve endings and muscle, triggering urgency, pain, and frequency signals.

Step 3 — Activation

Mast Cell & Nerve Activation

Suburothelial mast cells degranulate, releasing histamine, heparin, and tryptase. Unmyelinated C-fibers become sensitized, reducing the pain threshold. This neurogenic inflammation perpetuates a self-sustaining cycle of urgency and hypersensitivity.

Step 4 — Chronicity

Central Sensitization

Chronic peripheral input leads to central sensitization in the spinal cord and brain. Pain becomes disproportionate to peripheral stimuli. This explains why IC/BPS often co-exists with fibromyalgia, IBS, and other central sensitivity syndromes.

Step 5 — Structural

Detrusor Fibrosis

In advanced or untreated disease, chronic inflammation leads to detrusor muscle fibrosis, reducing bladder capacity. Hunner's lesions — distinctive mucosal ulcerations — may develop in a subpopulation of IC/BPS patients (~10%), representing a more severe phenotype.

Step 6 — Overlap

Comorbidity Cascade

IC/BPS rarely exists in isolation. Pelvic floor dysfunction, endometriosis, irritable bowel syndrome, and vulvodynia frequently co-occur. This complex interplay requires multidisciplinary management and an individualized treatment approach.

Clinical Presentation

Recognizing the Symptoms

Bladder sensitivity symptoms in women are frequently under-recognized in routine practice. Early identification and timely intervention can prevent progression to chronicity.

HIGH

Bladder Pain / Pelvic Pain

Persistent pelvic pain, pressure, or discomfort, often worsening as the bladder fills. Pain may radiate to the lower abdomen, urethra, vagina, or lower back. The hallmark symptom of IC/BPS.

HIGH

Urinary Urgency

Sudden, compelling need to urinate — often severe and not relieved by emptying. Patients may void up to 60 times per day in severe cases, profoundly impacting daily function.

MOD-HIGH

Dysuria / Burning Sensation

Burning or stinging pain during or after urination. Often confused with bacterial cystitis but without microbiological evidence of infection. Persistent and recurrent in nature.

MOD-HIGH

Urinary Frequency

Urinating more than 8 times per day with small volumes, driven by pain and urgency rather than true incontinence. Nocturia — waking more than twice per night — is common.

MODERATE

Suprapubic Pain & Pressure

Discomfort above the pubic bone, often described as a heaviness or bloating sensation. May present subtly in early disease before evolving to more severe pelvic pain.

MODERATE

Strangury

Slow, painful urination — a feeling of wanting to urinate with only small amounts passing despite continued effort. Results from bladder wall inflammation and detrusor spasm.

VARIABLE

Dyspareunia

Pain during or after sexual intercourse, particularly in women with pelvic floor involvement. Significantly impacts quality of life, relationships, and psychological wellbeing.

VARIABLE

Psychological Impact

Chronic pain and urgency lead to anxiety, depression, and social isolation. Sleep disturbance from nocturia further compounds quality of life impairment. Psychological support is integral to comprehensive care.

Clinical Assessment

Diagnosis of IC / Bladder Pain Syndrome

IC/BPS is a diagnosis of exclusion. The diagnostic journey typically involves ruling out infection, malignancy, and other structural causes before confirming the clinical diagnosis based on symptom criteria.

  • 1

    Symptom Assessment

    Detailed history of bladder pain, urgency, frequency, and impact on quality of life. Use validated tools: O'Leary-Sant IC Symptom Index (ICSI) and Problem Index (ICPI).

  • 2

    Urine Analysis & Culture

    Rule out bacterial cystitis (MSU culture), STIs, and other infective causes. Negative cultures in the presence of significant symptoms point toward IC/BPS.

  • 3

    Potassium Sensitivity Test (PST)

    Intravesical instillation of potassium solution — positive test (pain/urgency with KCl but not saline) suggests compromised GAG layer and supports IC/BPS diagnosis.

  • 4

    Cystoscopy with Hydrodistension

    Under anaesthesia, identifies Hunner's lesions (present in ~10% of IC/BPS), glomerulations (petechial bleeding post-distension), and reduced bladder capacity.

  • 5

    Bladder Biopsy

    Reserved for cases with Hunner's lesions or to exclude carcinoma in situ. Demonstrates increased mast cells, submucosal fibrosis, and inflammatory infiltrates in IC/BPS.

  • 6

    Voiding Diary

    3-day voiding diary documents frequency, urgency, volumes, and pain scores. Provides objective baseline data and guides treatment response monitoring.

ESSIC Diagnostic Criteria for IC/BPS

— Chronic pelvic pain, pressure, or discomfort perceived to be related to the urinary bladder
— At least one other urinary symptom: persistent urgency of micturition or increased urinary frequency
— Duration of symptoms for more than 6 weeks
— Absence of confusable diseases (infection, malignancy, urolithiasis, urethral diverticulum)
— NIDDK criteria: Glomerulations on cystoscopy OR Hunner's lesions AND pain with bladder filling OR urinary urgency

Red Flags — Refer Urgently

! Haematuria (visible or non-visible) — exclude malignancy first
! Symptoms in women over 50 with smoking history — bladder cancer risk
! New symptoms post-pelvic radiotherapy — radiation cystitis or fistula
! Rapidly worsening symptoms — may indicate advanced pathology

Management Approach

Dietary & Lifestyle Modification 1st Line

Elimination of dietary triggers is foundational. Acidic foods (citrus, tomatoes, vinegar), caffeine, carbonated drinks, and spicy foods commonly exacerbate symptoms. The IC Diet — elimination followed by systematic reintroduction — identifies individual triggers effectively.

Stress management, pelvic floor physiotherapy, and bladder retraining exercises (gradually extending voiding intervals) form the core of conservative management and can reduce symptoms by up to 50% when adhered to consistently.

Oral Pharmacotherapy 2nd Line

Pentosan Polysulfate Sodium (Elmiron) — the only FDA-approved oral agent for IC/BPS — replenishes the defective GAG layer. Dosed at 100mg three times daily on an empty stomach for 3–6 months for optimal response.

Adjunctive agents include tricyclic antidepressants (amitriptyline — pain modulation and anticholinergic effect), antihistamines (hydroxyzine — mast cell stabilization), and cimetidine (H2 antagonist with anti-inflammatory properties).

Intravesical Instillation Therapy 3rd Line

Direct bladder instillation delivers therapeutic agents to the urothelium, bypassing systemic absorption. Elmiron Instill (PPS 10mg/ml) — the world's first CDSCO-approved intravesical PPS — instilled at 30mL retained for 30–60 minutes, once or twice weekly for 8–12 weeks.

The DMSO (dimethyl sulfoxide) cocktail remains widely used: DMSO + heparin + hydrocortisone + sodium bicarbonate. Heparin instillation alone is also effective. Lidocaine instillation provides immediate short-term symptom relief.

Interventional Procedures 4th Line

Cystoscopy with hydrodistension under anaesthesia both diagnoses and provides short-term symptom relief (70–80% respond, lasting 4–6 months). Hunner's lesions are treated with laser ablation or steroid injection at the time of cystoscopy.

Sacral neuromodulation (InterStim) is FDA-approved for refractory IC/BPS, modulating aberrant bladder sensory pathways. Pudendal nerve stimulation may offer superior outcomes in some patients.

Surgical Management 5th Line

Reserved for severe, refractory disease unresponsive to all conservative and pharmacological measures. Options include augmentation cystoplasty (bladder augmentation with bowel segment) and, in the most severe cases, urinary diversion with or without cystectomy.

Surgical outcomes are variable and patient selection is critical. Multidisciplinary review including urology, pain management, and psychological support is essential prior to any surgical intervention.
